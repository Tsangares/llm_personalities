# Human behavioral benchmarks for classic game theory experiments

Experimental economics has produced robust quantitative benchmarks for comparing human behavior to AI and LLM decision-making across classic game theory paradigms. This report synthesizes meta-analyses and large-sample studies spanning over 100,000 total human decisions, providing specific means, distributions, sample sizes, and citations suitable for systematic LLM behavioral comparisons.

## Dictator Game: humans give 28% on average, but distributions reveal deep heterogeneity

The Dictator Game provides the cleanest measure of pure altruistic preferences, as the proposer simply allocates an endowment between themselves and a recipient with no strategic considerations. **The most comprehensive benchmark comes from Engel (2011), who meta-analyzed 129 studies with 20,813 observations spanning 25 years and found mean giving of 28.35% of endowment** (standard deviation approximately 25-35 percentage points). This figure is remarkably robust across laboratories, time periods, and experimenters in WEIRD populations.

The distribution reveals profound individual heterogeneity with a striking bimodal pattern. **36.11% of dictators give nothing (pure selfishness), while 16.74% split equally (50/50), and just 5.44% give everything**. The remaining 42% distribute across the middle range, creating a relatively flat distribution between the two peaks at 0% and 50%. Critically, 64% give positive amounts, demonstrating that most humans exhibit at least some other-regarding preferences even in completely anonymous settings.

Forsythe et al. (1994) established the foundational benchmark with their seminal study, finding mean giving around 20% with 60% giving positive amounts. The difference between their 20% and Engel's 28% reflects the evolution of experimental protocols—early studies often used less anonymity, which paradoxically increased selfish behavior as subjects worried about experimenter judgment.

Context dramatically shifts behavior. **Under strict double-blind anonymity, the majority keep everything and almost all keep at least 80%**, effectively eliminating the social signaling component (Hoffman et al. 1994, 1996). When List (2007) introduced a "taking" frame where dictators could take money from recipients, giving dropped from 71% of subjects to just 10%, suggesting standard framing artificially inflates generosity. Conversely, **cross-cultural studies of non-student adults in small-scale societies show mean giving of 40-50%** with modal offers at 50% (Henrich et al. 2005, studying 15 societies).

Stake size effects are modest but consistent. Larney, Rotella, and Barclay (2019) meta-analyzed 31 studies with 3,233 participants and found a small negative effect (Cohen's d = 0.15)—proportional giving decreases as stakes increase, though the effect strengthens when comparing more extreme stake differences.

Gender patterns show nuance. Meta-analyses by Bilén et al. (2021) and Doñate-Buendía et al. (2022) examining 136 conditions found **women are more generous than men on average at moderate to large social distances**, but this reverses with close friends and family. The effect also varies geographically, with women giving more in the Americas and Oceania but less in South Africa. Andreoni and Vesterlund (2001) demonstrated that gender differences depend on the price of giving—women prioritize equality while men prioritize efficiency.

Preference heterogeneity follows predictable patterns. Andreoni and Miller (2002) used revealed preference methods to classify participants, finding approximately 15-20% are purely selfish, 30-35% are Leontief egalitarians (minimize inequality), 15-20% are perfect substitutes utilitarians, with the remainder exhibiting mixed preferences. Critically, **81-85% of participants exhibit GARP consistency**, indicating these preferences reflect stable utility functions rather than random noise.

**Key citations:** Engel (2011) in *Experimental Economics* for the primary meta-analysis; Forsythe et al. (1994) in *Games and Economic Behavior* for the foundational study; Larney et al. (2019) in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* for stake effects; Henrich et al. (2005) in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* for cross-cultural variation; Andreoni and Miller (2002) in *Econometrica* for preference types; List (2007) in *Journal of Political Economy* for framing effects.

## Ultimatum Game: offers cluster at 40-50% with sharp rejection thresholds

The Ultimatum Game adds strategic considerations—proposers offer a split of an endowment, and responders accept or reject (both receiving nothing if rejected). This combination of fairness concerns and strategic reasoning produces tight behavioral convergence around equal splits.

**Oosterbeek, Sloof, and van de Kuilen (2004) meta-analyzed 37 papers with 75 results and found mean proposer offers of 40% of the pie with an overall rejection rate of 16%**. Tisserand (2014) analyzed 97 observations from 42 articles (1983-2012) and confirmed a weighted average offer of 42.3%. The distribution is highly concentrated: **60-80% of offers fall in the 40-50% range, with the modal offer at exactly 50%** (equal split). Virtually no offers exceed 50%, and only 3-8% fall below 20%.

Rejection behavior follows a predictable threshold pattern. Offers of 50% are virtually always accepted (0-5% rejection). **Offers of 40-49% face approximately 20% rejection, 30-39% face 30% rejection, and offers below 20% are rejected 50-70% of the time**. This pattern reflects underlying inequality aversion—Fehr and Schmidt (1999) demonstrated that approximately 30% of responders have acceptance thresholds around 25% (accepting any offer above one-quarter), another 30% around 33%, and 10% require offers above 44% to accept.

Across 875 observations compiled by Fehr and Schmidt (1999) from multiple seminal studies, **71% of offers fell in the 40-50% range while only 3.8% were below 20%**. This concentration is remarkable given that game theory predicts proposers should offer the minimum possible amount, which responders should accept.

Cross-cultural variation exists but primarily in responder rather than proposer behavior. Roth et al. (1991) compared USA, Slovenia, Israel, and Japan (N=116) and found similar mean offers (40-45%) across all countries, but rejection rates varied with higher rejections in Slovenia and the USA versus Japan and Israel. **The most dramatic cultural effect appears in Henrich's (2000) study of the Machiguenga in Peru, showing mean offers of just 26% with modal offers at 15% and low rejection rates** even for unfair offers. Henrich et al. (2001) studied 15 small-scale societies and found offer ranges of 25-57%, with market integration and cooperation norms positively predicting higher offers.

Stake size has minimal effects. Larney et al. (2019) found almost zero effect of stakes on offers (d = 0.02) in their meta-analysis of 21 papers. Even in extreme high-stakes conditions—Cameron (1995) used stakes equivalent to three months' income in Indonesia, and Andersen et al. (2011) used stakes over 20,000 rupees—offers remained around 40% and rejection rates stayed substantial.

The robustness to experience is notable. Unlike many games where behavior converges toward Nash equilibrium with repetition, **Ultimatum Game offers stabilize around 40-45% even with extensive experience**. This suggests the fairness norms are deeply ingrained rather than resulting from confusion or learning.

**Key citations:** Oosterbeek et al. (2004) in *Experimental Economics* for the primary meta-analysis; Güth, Schmittberger, and Schwarze (1982) in *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* for the original study (N=79); Fehr and Schmidt (1999) in *Quarterly Journal of Economics* for the inequality aversion framework; Henrich (2000) in *American Economic Review* and Henrich et al. (2001) in *American Economic Review* for cross-cultural studies; Camerer (2003) *Behavioral Game Theory* for comprehensive review; Roth et al. (1991) in *American Economic Review* for four-country comparison.

## Public Goods Game: initial cooperation at 49% decays by 2.4 points per round

The linear Public Goods Game creates a social dilemma where individual and collective interests conflict—each player decides how much of an endowment to contribute to a group account that gets multiplied and shared equally. This structure cleanly captures cooperation versus free-riding tensions.

**Burton-Chellew and West (2020) conducted the largest meta-analysis to date, examining 237 games from 129 studies with 17,940 total participants**. They found **initial contributions average 49% of endowment (95% CI: 47.2-51.0%) in round one, declining by 2.4 percentage points per round** (95% CI: -2.64, -2.20). This decay pattern is remarkably consistent—conditional cooperators reduce contributions in response to free-riders, creating a self-reinforcing downward spiral.

The marginal per capita return (MPCR)—the individual benefit from each unit contributed to the public pool—strongly predicts behavior. Van den Berg et al. (2020) tested 12 MPCR levels with 648 participants and found contributions ranged from 38% declining to 17% at low MPCR (0.367), while high MPCR (0.833) sustained contributions from 68% declining to 61%. **Approximately 80% of the variance in contribution responses occurs below MPCR of 0.58, with contributions plateauing around 63% once MPCR exceeds 0.7**.

Zelmer's (2003) meta-analysis of 27 studies (711 groups) identified systematic factors affecting cooperation. **Communication opportunities, constant group composition ("partners" rather than random "strangers"), and positive framing all significantly increase contributions**, while heterogeneous endowments, experienced participants, and belief elicitation decrease contributions. Notably, group size per se had no significant independent effect when controlling for MPCR.

Individual heterogeneity follows the classification established by Fischbacher, Gächter, and Fehr (2001), who used the strategy method to identify types. **Approximately 50-58% are conditional cooperators who contribute more when others contribute more (though with a self-serving bias—they contribute slightly less than others' average), 12-30% are pure free-riders who contribute zero regardless, 10-13% show hump-shaped patterns, and around 9% are unconditional cooperators**. Burton-Chellew, El Mouden, and West (2016) challenged this interpretation by showing similar type distributions emerge when playing against computers, suggesting confusion contributes significantly to apparent cooperation.

The decay mechanism centers on learning. Burton-Chellew and West (2020) demonstrated that contributions decline faster when groups are smaller (lower N) or returns are lower (lower MPCR), because individual "influence" over payoffs is higher, facilitating learning that contribution is personally costly. The N×Round interaction was highly significant (F₁,₇₄.₉ = 10.8, P = 0.002), with smaller groups showing steeper decay curves.

Punishment dramatically alters behavior. Fehr and Gächter (2000) showed that when peer punishment is available—players can pay to reduce others' payoffs—cooperation stabilizes at high levels rather than decaying. This finding has generated thousands of citations and demonstrates that humans are willing to incur costs to enforce fairness norms.

**Key citations:** Burton-Chellew and West (2020) in *Nature Human Behaviour* for the largest meta-analysis; Fischbacher, Gächter, and Fehr (2001) in *Economics Letters* (2000+ citations) for conditional cooperation types; Zelmer (2003) in *Experimental Economics* (379+ citations) for comprehensive meta-analysis; Fehr and Gächter (2000) in *American Economic Review* (3000+ citations) for punishment effects; Ledyard (1995) in *Handbook of Experimental Economics* for foundational review; Van den Berg et al. (2020) in *Scientific Reports* for MPCR effects; Chaudhuri (2011) in *Experimental Economics* for selective survey.

## Trust Game: investors send 51% while trustees return 37% of tripled amounts

The Trust Game (also called Investment Game) measures both trust and trustworthiness in a sequential two-player interaction. The investor receives an endowment and decides how much to send to a trustee; the amount is multiplied (typically tripled), and the trustee decides how much to return.

**Johnson and Mislin (2011) conducted the definitive meta-analysis of 162 replications across 35 countries with 23,924 total participants**. They found **investors send an average of 50.9% of endowment (mean = 0.5087606, SD = 0.1377334), while trustees return an average of 36.5% of the amount available to return** (mean = 0.3651079, SD = 0.0942361). The range of observed behavior spans from 9.4% to 88.5% for sending and 17.0% to 55.7% for returns across different studies.

Berg, Dickhaut, and McCabe's (1995) seminal study established these benchmarks with 32 participants and a $10 endowment tripled to $30. They found average sending of $5.16 (51.6%) with average returns of $4.66, meaning **trustees returned more on average than investors originally sent, demonstrating strong reciprocity norms**. Critically, 30 of 32 trials (93.75%) involved positive amounts sent, and only 36% of trustors expected to receive more back than they sent, suggesting trust is motivated by social preferences rather than pure monetary calculation.

The distribution reveals that approximately **11% send nothing, while 89-94% send positive amounts, with modal behavior clustering around 50% of endowment**. For returns, about 20% give nothing back while 80% exhibit positive reciprocity, with return amounts strongly correlating with amounts received—trustees respond more generously to larger displays of trust.

Age significantly affects trustworthiness. Sutter and Kocher (2007) demonstrated that **trust increases linearly from childhood to early adulthood, while younger student participants exhibit significantly lower trustworthiness than non-student populations**. This finding challenges the widespread use of undergraduate samples as representative of general human behavior.

Cultural and institutional factors matter substantially. Johnson and Mislin (2011) found that **one standard deviation increase in ethnic fractionalization corresponds to approximately one-third standard deviation decrease in trustworthiness** (p < 0.05), with similar effects for income inequality measured by Gini coefficient. African participants sent significantly less than North American participants. However, some pairwise comparisons show remarkable similarity—Holm and Danielson (2005) found "remarkably similar" trust behavior between Tanzania and Sweden despite vast institutional differences.

Methodological factors affect behavior systematically. **Higher stakes decrease amounts sent but increase amounts returned**. When receivers are endowed with their own initial funds, both trust and trustworthiness increase. **Student subjects send more but return less than non-students** (significant at p < 0.01), suggesting students are more trusting but less trustworthy. The strategy method (eliciting conditional return schedules) and playing both roles sequentially both reduce returns. Surprisingly, double-blind protocols show no significant effect, unlike in the Dictator Game.

The variance decomposition reveals that **approximately 40% of variance in trust and 30% of variance in trustworthiness can be explained by experimental protocol variations** (Johnson & Mislin 2011), indicating substantial researcher degrees of freedom and the importance of standardized designs for fair comparisons.

**Key citations:** Johnson and Mislin (2011) in *Journal of Economic Psychology* for the comprehensive meta-analysis (162 studies, 23,924 participants); Berg, Dickhaut, and McCabe (1995) in *Games and Economic Behavior* (4500+ citations) for the original study; Camerer (2003) *Behavioral Game Theory* for extensive review; Fehr (2009) in *Journal of the European Economic Association* for trust mechanisms; Buchan et al. (2002) for US-China-Japan-Korea comparisons; Cronk (2007) in *Evolution and Human Behavior* for cultural framing effects with Maasai; Sutter and Kocher (2007) in *Games and Economic Behavior* for age effects.

## Prisoner's Dilemma: initial cooperation 45-80% with systematic end-game unraveling

Finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma experiments test whether humans cooperate despite backward induction logic predicting universal defection. The evidence reveals substantial initial cooperation that systematically erodes as the end approaches, though the speed and extent of decay varies with game parameters and player types.

**Embrey, Fréchette, and Yuksel (2018) conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of over 50 years of research plus new experiments with 200+ subjects playing 20-30 finitely repeated games**. They resolved contradictory prior results by demonstrating that **subjects converge to threshold strategies—cooperating until round X, then defecting—with the threshold systematically moving earlier with experience**. Despite theoretical predictions of immediate defection, game parameters significantly affect initial cooperation rates.

The most detailed behavioral data comes from **Mao et al. (2017), who tracked 94 subjects over 20 consecutive weekdays playing 400 ten-round games each, yielding 374,251 total decisions**. On day one, **round one cooperation exceeded 80%, declining to approximately 75% by round eight, 65% by round nine, and 55% by round ten**. This clear end-game effect appeared immediately. During days 1-6, cooperation in final rounds decreased steadily as the modal round of first defection moved earlier—the classic unraveling pattern.

Remarkably, after day 7, **cooperation stabilized at over 84% across all rounds and remained stable through day 20**. This stabilization occurred because the population segregated into two types: **40% "resilient cooperators" who consistently played conditional cooperation (cooperate until partner defects) even when exploited, and 60% "rational players" who exhibited systematic unraveling but converged on threshold strategies averaging first defection around round 8.2**. Simulations predicted this cooperation level could be sustained indefinitely with just 10% resilient cooperators—a critical mass threshold (α* ≈ 0.1).

Sally's (1995) meta-analysis of 35 years (1958-1992) across 130+ treatment conditions from multiple disciplines established that **baseline cooperation in anonymous settings ranges from 40-60% in one-shot games, increasing by approximately 45 percentage points when discussion is allowed**. In repeated games, communication increases cooperation by around 40 percentage points, demonstrating the power of even minimal social interaction.

End-game effects are ubiquitous and robust. **Cooperation typically drops 15-25 percentage points from round nine to round one in ten-round games, and 25-40 percentage points comparing round ten to round one**. However, "restart effects" occur—cooperation jumps sharply from the last round of game j to the first round of game j+1. Between-session restart effects are particularly large (+17.2% for round nine, +13.6% for round ten in Mao et al. 2017), suggesting overnight memory consolidation or renewed optimism.

The rate of unraveling depends critically on payoff parameters. In Mao et al.'s experiment with T=7, R=5, P=3, S=1 (normalized g=1, l=1—relatively low temptation and sucker payoffs), high cooperation was sustainable. Games with higher temptation (g) or loss from being suckered (l) show faster unraveling. Partner matching (playing the same opponent repeatedly) versus stranger matching (random rematching each round) also matters substantially—fixed pairings can increase cooperation by 20-40 percentage points (Duffy & Ochs experiments).

Personality predicts behavior. Studies examining Big Five traits found that **one standard deviation increase in Agreeableness increases predicted cooperation probability from 67.9% to 80.6%**. Intelligence also matters—**SAT scores show 5-8% more cooperation for every 100-point increase**, suggesting higher cognitive ability aids recognition of long-term cooperation benefits.

Communication effects are dramatic. When unrestricted pre-play discussion is allowed, **stage-one cooperation reaches 95-100%** compared to baselines of 57-62% without communication (Kagel 2018). Analysis of the British game show "Golden Balls"—essentially a high-stakes one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with average stakes of £13,300—showed unilateral cooperation rates of 55% and mutual cooperation of 33%, with higher stakes negatively correlated with cooperation.

**Key citations:** Mao et al. (2017) in *Nature Communications* for the most comprehensive longitudinal data (374,251 decisions); Embrey, Fréchette, and Yuksel (2018) in *Quarterly Journal of Economics* for meta-analysis and threshold strategy framework; Sally (1995) in *Rationality and Society* for meta-analysis of 35 years (130+ conditions); Selten and Stoecker (1986) in *Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization* for pioneering end-game documentation; Andreoni and Miller (1993) in *Economic Journal* for sequential equilibrium and reputation effects; Roth and Murnighan (1978) in *Journal of Mathematical Psychology* and (1983) in *Journal of Conflict Resolution* for early experimental foundations.

## Volunteer's Dilemma: individual rates decline but group success increases with size

The Volunteer's Dilemma captures scenarios where at least one person must volunteer (incurring a cost) to provide a benefit to everyone, but if multiple volunteer, costs are duplicated unnecessarily. This game has substantially less experimental literature than others but reveals counterintuitive patterns contradicting Nash predictions.

**The most systematic evidence comes from three large-scale studies.** Goeree, Holt, and Smith (2017) tested groups of N = 2, 3, 6, 9, and 12 in repeated games (20 rounds, random matching) with benefits V = $1.00, cost C = $0.20, and no-volunteer payoff L = $0.20. **They found volunteering rates decrease with group size as theory predicts, but critically, success rates (at least one volunteer) increase with size, contradicting Nash equilibrium predictions**. Volunteer rates were too low for small groups (N=2,3) compared to Nash predictions but slightly above Nash for larger groups (N=6,9,12).

Kopányi-Peuker (2019) conducted a large one-shot experiment with 648 subjects across very different group sizes (N = 3, 15, 39, ~100) and two cost levels (50% and 80% of benefit). The findings reveal **non-monotonic group size effects**: for low cost (50%), volunteering rates were 31% (N=3), 20% (N=15), 23% (N=39), and 21% (N≈100). For high cost (80%), rates were 20% (N=3), 11% (N=15), 23% (N=39), and 18% (N≈100). **Critically, success rates were 67% for low-cost N=3, but 100% for all groups N≥15**.

The cost effect shows striking patterns. **Costs significantly affect volunteering only in small groups (N=3), where high costs (80% of benefit) reduce volunteering from 31% to 20% (p=0.07). For all groups N≥15, cost effects disappear (p>0.10)**. This suggests that strategic reasoning about costs weakens in larger groups where diffusion of responsibility dominates.

Franzen (1995) tested eight group sizes from N=2 to N=101 with 203 subjects via questionnaire methodology. **Volunteer rates fell from approximately 67% for small groups to 50% for large groups, but the relationship was non-monotonic rather than perfectly linear**. Crucially, the probability of getting at least one volunteer approached 1 for groups with 10+ members, contradicting Nash predictions that success probability should decline and approach 0.75 as N→∞.

The classic behavioral foundations come from Darley and Latané's (1968) bystander effect study, where subjects encountered a staged medical emergency. **With one bystander alone, 100% helped. With one other bystander present (N=2), 82% helped. With five others (N=5), just 62% helped**—clear evidence of diffusion of responsibility. Diekmann (1985, 1986) introduced the game-theoretic volunteer's dilemma framework to formalize these effects, citing Darley and Latané's data.

Gender effects are significant. **Women volunteer substantially more than men overall (p<0.01), with particularly strong effects in small groups (N=3, p<0.01)**. In Kopányi-Peuker's study, women volunteered at 31% while men volunteered at lower rates in small groups. Economics students volunteer significantly less than non-economics students, suggesting game theory training shifts behavior toward more strategic free-riding.

The theoretical disconnect is profound. **Mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium predicts both individual volunteering rates and success rates should decline with group size**. Reality shows individual rates decline but success rates increase, with large groups (N≥15) achieving near-perfect success. Goeree, Holt, and Smith (2017) demonstrate that Quantal Response Equilibrium (QRE)—which incorporates behavioral noise/errors—fits the data far better than Nash, predicting success rates approach 1 for sufficiently large groups.

Otsubo and Rapoport (2008) examined dynamic volunteer's dilemmas where participants can volunteer at different times over a finite horizon (N=9 groups). They found subjects **volunteer earlier than predicted by theory and show significant cost sensitivity**—lower costs generate more volunteering. However, coordinating on asymmetric equilibria (where exactly one person volunteers with certainty) proves difficult without communication.

**Key citations:** Goeree, Holt, and Smith (2017) in *Games and Economic Behavior* for systematic group size effects; Kopányi-Peuker (2019) in *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics* for large-scale study (648 subjects); Diekmann (1985) in *Journal of Conflict Resolution* (374+ citations) for introducing the game; Darley and Latané (1968) in *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* for classic bystander effect; Franzen (1995) in *Rationality and Society* for group sizes up to 101; Otsubo and Rapoport (2008) in *Journal of Conflict Resolution* for dynamic games; Murnighan, Kim, and Metzger (1993) in *Administrative Science Quarterly* for organizational applications.

## Critical methodological considerations for LLM comparisons

Several factors must be considered when comparing LLM behavior to these human benchmarks. First, **student samples dominate this literature, but students are not representative**—they are younger, more educated, from WEIRD populations (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic), and show systematically different behavior than general populations (typically less generous in Dictator Games, less trustworthy in Trust Games).

Second, **stakes matter differently across games**. Dictator Game giving decreases modestly with stakes (d=0.15), but Ultimatum Game offers remain stable even at stakes equivalent to three months' income. Public Goods contributions show small stake effects. This suggests that social norm strength varies—strong norms (like fairness in Ultimatum Games) resist stake manipulation, while weaker norms (like generosity in Dictator Games) show stake sensitivity.

Third, **framing effects are substantial**. The same strategic structure produces dramatically different behavior under "giving" versus "taking" frames (Dictator Game), "contribution" versus "withdrawal" frames (Public Goods), and different contextual labels. LLMs should be tested under multiple framings to assess frame sensitivity.

Fourth, **experience and learning effects differ by game**. Public Goods contributions decay systematically with repetition, Prisoner's Dilemma cooperation shows unraveling with experience, but Ultimatum Game offers remain stable around 40-50% even after extensive repetition. This suggests some behavioral patterns reflect stable preferences while others involve learning dynamics.

Fifth, **communication transforms behavior universally**. Sally (1995) documented 40-45 percentage point increases in cooperation across social dilemmas when discussion is permitted. Since LLMs can process language, comparing behavior with and without communication opportunities is essential.

Finally, **protocol variations explain 30-40% of variance in Trust Game behavior** (Johnson & Mislin 2011), with similar researcher degrees of freedom in other games. Standardized protocols are critical for valid comparisons. Key protocol elements include anonymity level (single-blind, double-blind), payment method (all decisions paid, random selection, hypothetical), role assignment (one role, both roles, strategy method), matching procedure (partners, strangers, one-shot), and information feedback.

## Recommended benchmark values for LLM behavioral comparisons

For standardized one-shot anonymous laboratory conditions with monetary incentives and student populations, the following benchmarks represent consensus values from the largest meta-analyses:

**Dictator Game**: Mean giving 28%, with 36% giving zero, 17% giving exactly half, and 64% giving positive amounts (Engel 2011, N=20,813).

**Ultimatum Game**: Mean proposer offer 40-42%, with 60-80% of offers in the 40-50% range. Rejection rates: 0-5% for 50%, ~20% for 40-49%, ~50% for 20-29% (Oosterbeek et al. 2004, N=875 aggregated).

**Public Goods Game**: Initial contribution 49% declining by 2.4 percentage points per round. Player types: 50-58% conditional cooperators, 12-30% free riders, 9% unconditional cooperators (Burton-Chellew & West 2020, N=17,940).

**Trust Game**: Investors send 51% of endowment, trustees return 37% of tripled amount. Approximately 11% send nothing, 94% send positive amounts (Johnson & Mislin 2011, N=23,924).

**Repeated Prisoner's Dilemma**: Initial cooperation 70-80% for 10-round games, declining to 55% in final round. Threshold strategies emerge with experience, with 40% maintaining conditional cooperation indefinitely (Mao et al. 2017, N=374,251 decisions).

**Volunteer's Dilemma**: Small groups (N=3) show 20-31% volunteering depending on cost, large groups (N≥15) show 11-23% volunteering with near-perfect group success rates. Women volunteer more than men (Kopányi-Peuker 2019, N=648; Goeree et al. 2017).

These benchmarks provide precise quantitative targets for evaluating whether LLMs exhibit human-like preferences, strategic reasoning, and social behavior across the canonical paradigms of experimental economics.
